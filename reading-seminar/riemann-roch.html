The Riemann–Roch theorem — Hartshorne IV.1

source: Hartshorne, Algebraic Geometry, IV.1  ·  used by: IV.2 Hurwitz, IV.3 Embeddings

This section first collects the notation and the facts about curves that will be needed, then states and proves the Riemann–Roch theorem, and finally uses it to derive a few concrete consequences. Hartshorne notes at the start of the chapter that, apart from the proof of Riemann–Roch (1.3) which uses Serre duality, almost nothing in the chapter requires the advanced machinery of schemes and cohomology; so a reader willing to take the statement of the theorem on faith can read this chapter quite early. A companion supplement treats, one by one, the notions that the main text passes over quickly but that tend to block you when you actually try to use them.

1. Curves: definition and conventions

Throughout this chapter, a curve means a complete nonsingular curve over an algebraically closed field $k$. In the language of (II, §6): a curve is an integral scheme, proper over $k$, of dimension $1$, all of whose local rings are regular. Such a curve is necessarily projective (II, 6.7), so the whole apparatus of II.7 on projective embeddings is available directly.

When more general objects are meant, we say “scheme” together with the appropriate qualifiers, for example “an integral scheme of dimension $1$ of finite type over $k$”. We also agree that point means closed point, unless the generic point is explicitly intended.

2. The genus

The single most important invariant of a curve is its genus. It has several definitions, all equivalent.

For a curve $X$ in projective space, the arithmetic genus is defined by $p_a(X)=1-P_X(0)$, where $P_X$ is the Hilbert polynomial of $X$ (I, Ex. 7.2). On the other hand the geometric genus is defined by $p_g(X)=\dim_k\Gamma(X,\omega_X)$, where $\omega_X$ is the canonical sheaf (II, 8.18.2).

Proposition 1.1 If $X$ is a curve, then $$p_a(X)=p_g(X)=\dim_k H^1(X,\mathcal{O}_X).$$ We therefore call this number simply the genus of $X$ and write it $g$.

Proof

The equality $p_a(X)=\dim H^1(X,\mathcal{O}_X)$ is (III, Ex. 5.3); the equality $p_g=\dim H^1(X,\mathcal{O}_X)$ is a consequence of Serre duality (III, 7.12.2). $\blacksquare$

Remark The three definitions look at one number from three directions: the Hilbert polynomial comes from the combinatorial data of a projective embedding, $p_g$ counts global differential forms, and $H^1$ is a cohomological dimension. The statement “the genus is the number of holes” on a compact Riemann surface corresponds to the $p_g$ branch, that is, to the dimension of the space of global holomorphic $1$-forms.

Remark 1.1.1 Since $g=p_g$, the genus of a curve is always nonnegative. Conversely, for every $g\geq0$ there is a curve of genus $g$: on a nonsingular quadric surface take a divisor of type $(g+1,2)$; among such divisors there are irreducible nonsingular ones, and these have $p_a=g$ (III, Ex. 5.6).

The nonsingular quadric surface used here is $Q\cong\mathbb{P}^1\times\mathbb{P}^1$, with $\operatorname{Pic}Q\cong\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}$ and divisors classified by their type $(a,b)$. In (II, 7.6.2) it was used to illustrate the ampleness criterion; here it becomes the source of curves of arbitrary genus.

3. Divisors and linear systems, recalled

A (Weil) divisor on a curve $X$ is an element of the free abelian group generated by the points of $X$, written $D=\sum n_iP_i$ with $n_i\in\mathbb{Z}$, and its degree is $\deg D=\sum n_i$. Two divisors are linearly equivalent if their difference is the divisor of a rational function; the degree of a divisor depends only on its linear equivalence class (II, 6.10).

Since $X$ is nonsingular, every divisor $D$ has an associated invertible sheaf $\mathscr{L}(D)$, and the correspondence $D\mapsto\mathscr{L}(D)$ gives a group isomorphism $$\operatorname{Cl}(X)\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }\operatorname{Pic}X$$ (II, 6.16).

A divisor $D=\sum n_iP_i$ is effective if all $n_i\geq0$. The set of effective divisors linearly equivalent to a given $D$ is the complete linear system $|D|$ (II, §7). The elements of $|D|$ are in bijection with $$\big(H^0(X,\mathscr{L}(D))-\{0\}\big)/k^*,$$ so $|D|$ carries the structure of the set of closed points of a projective space (II, 7.7). Writing $l(D)=\dim_k H^0(X,\mathscr{L}(D))$, we have $$\dim|D|=l(D)-1.$$ That $l(D)$ is finite follows from (II, 5.19) or (III, 5.2).

Remark The bijection here is exactly the content of (II, 7.7): the divisor of zeros of a section gives an effective divisor, and two sections have the same divisor of zeros precisely when they differ by a nonzero constant. The “minus one” in $\dim|D|=l(D)-1$ comes from projectivizing a vector space; forgetting it is the single most common error when computing with Riemann–Roch later on.

4. A basic lemma

Lemma 1.2 Let $D$ be a divisor on a curve $X$. If $l(D)\neq0$, then $\deg D\geq0$. If moreover $l(D)\neq0$ and $\deg D=0$, then $D\sim0$, that is $\mathscr{L}(D)\cong\mathcal{O}_X$.

Proof

If $l(D)\neq0$ then $|D|$ is nonempty, so $D$ is linearly equivalent to an effective divisor. The degree depends only on the linear equivalence class, and effective divisors have nonnegative degree, so $\deg D\geq0$. If in addition $\deg D=0$, then $D$ is linearly equivalent to an effective divisor of degree $0$; the only such divisor is the zero divisor, so $D\sim0$. $\blacksquare$

Remark In practice the contrapositive is what gets used: $\deg D<0$ implies $l(D)=0$. Every time the term $l(K-D)$ in Riemann–Roch needs to be killed, this is what does it.

5. The canonical divisor

Write $\Omega_{X/k}$ (abbreviated $\Omega_X$) for the sheaf of relative differentials of $X$ over $k$ (II, §8). Since $X$ has dimension $1$, $\Omega_X$ is an invertible sheaf on $X$ and therefore coincides with the canonical sheaf $\omega_X$. Any divisor in the corresponding linear equivalence class is called a canonical divisor and is denoted $K$.

The letter $K$ is sometimes also used for the function field of $X$, but the context distinguishes them.

Remark Dimension $1$ is the crucial point here. In dimension $n$ the sheaf $\Omega_X$ is locally free of rank $n$ and the canonical sheaf is its top exterior power; on a curve the rank is exactly $1$ and the two coincide. So on a curve, differential forms and the canonical line bundle are the same object, and $K$ can be treated either as a differential form or as a divisor, to be added, subtracted and given a degree.

6. The Riemann–Roch theorem

Theorem 1.3 (Riemann–Roch) Let $D$ be a divisor on a curve $X$ of genus $g$. Then $$l(D)-l(K-D)=\deg D+1-g.$$

Proof

The idea is to use Serre duality to replace the left-hand side by an Euler characteristic, and then induct on $D$ one point at a time.

The divisor $K-D$ corresponds to the invertible sheaf $\omega_X\otimes\mathscr{L}(D)^{\vee}$. Since $X$ is projective (II, 6.7), Serre duality (III, 7.12.1) applies and says that the vector spaces $H^0(X,\omega_X\otimes\mathscr{L}(D)^{\vee})$ and $H^1(X,\mathscr{L}(D))$ are dual. Hence $l(K-D)=\dim H^1(X,\mathscr{L}(D))$, and the statement to be proved becomes: for every $D$, $$\chi(\mathscr{L}(D))=\deg D+1-g,$$ where for a coherent sheaf $\mathscr{F}$ on $X$ the Euler characteristic is $\chi(\mathscr{F})=\dim H^0(X,\mathscr{F})-\dim H^1(X,\mathscr{F})$.

Take $D=0$ first. Here we must show $\dim H^0(X,\mathcal{O}_X)-\dim H^1(X,\mathcal{O}_X)=0+1-g$. This is correct, because on a projective variety $H^0(X,\mathcal{O}_X)=k$ (I, 3.4), while $\dim H^1(X,\mathcal{O}_X)=g$ by (1.1).

Next we show that for any divisor $D$ and any point $P$, the formula holds for $D$ if and only if it holds for $D+P$. Since any divisor can be reached from $0$ in finitely many steps, adding or subtracting one point at a time, this settles all $D$.

Regard $P$ as a closed subscheme of $X$; its structure sheaf is the skyscraper sheaf $k(P)$ at $P$, and its ideal sheaf is $\mathscr{L}(-P)$ (II, 6.18). This gives the exact sequence $$0\to\mathscr{L}(-P)\to\mathcal{O}_X\to k(P)\to 0.$$ Tensoring with $\mathscr{L}(D+P)$ gives $$0\to\mathscr{L}(D)\to\mathscr{L}(D+P)\to k(P)\to 0.$$ The third term is unchanged, because $\mathscr{L}(D+P)$ is locally free of rank $1$ and tensoring by it does not affect the sheaf $k(P)$. The Euler characteristic is additive on short exact sequences (III, Ex. 5.1), and $\chi(k(P))=1$, so $$\chi(\mathscr{L}(D+P))=\chi(\mathscr{L}(D))+1.$$ On the other hand $\deg(D+P)=\deg D+1$. Both sides increase by $1$ together, so the formula holds for $D$ if and only if it holds for $D+P$. $\blacksquare$

Remark The substance of the proof is just two things: Serre duality reinterprets $l(K-D)$ as $\dim H^1$, which turns the statement into a linear identity about $\chi$; and the change in $\chi$ when a divisor is modified by one point is exactly $1$, matching the change in $\deg$. The genuinely deep part lives inside Serre duality; the derivation of Riemann–Roch itself is formal.

Remark 1.3.1 If $X$ is a curve of degree $d$ in $\mathbb{P}^n$ and $D$ is a hyperplane section $X\cap H$, that is $\mathscr{L}(D)=\mathcal{O}_X(1)$, then the Hilbert polynomial (III, Ex. 5.2) gives $$\chi(\mathscr{L}(D))=d+1-p_a.$$ This is a special case of Riemann–Roch.

Remark 1.3.2 Riemann–Roch settles the “Riemann–Roch problem” on curves (II, Ex. 7.6), namely determining $\dim|nD|$ as a function of $n$. If $\deg D<0$, then $\dim|nD|=-1$ for all $n>0$. If $\deg D=0$, then $\dim|nD|$ is $0$ or $-1$ according as $nD\sim0$ or not. If $\deg D>0$, then as soon as $n\cdot\deg D>\deg K$ we have $l(K-nD)=0$ by (1.2), so for $n\gg0$ $$\dim|nD|=n\cdot\deg D-g.$$

7. Immediate consequences

Example 1.3.3 On a curve of genus $g$, the canonical divisor $K$ has degree $2g-2$.

Apply Riemann–Roch with $D=K$. Here $l(K)=p_g=g$ and $l(0)=1$, so $$g-1=\deg K+1-g,$$ that is $\deg K=2g-2$.

Example 1.3.4 A divisor $D$ is called special if $l(K-D)>0$, and $l(K-D)$ is its index of speciality; otherwise $D$ is nonspecial. If $\deg D>2g-2$, then $\deg(K-D)<0$ by (1.3.3), so $l(K-D)=0$ by (1.2), that is, $D$ is nonspecial.

Remark Nonspeciality is a convenient state to be in: there Riemann–Roch gives $l(D)=\deg D+1-g$ outright, and the dimension is completely determined by the degree. For special divisors this fails, and $l(D)$ depends on more than $\deg D$ — which is exactly the situation Clifford's theorem (5.4) will handle.

Example 1.3.5 A curve $X$ is rational (that is, birational to $\mathbb{P}^1$) if and only if $g=0$.

Since the curves of this chapter are complete and nonsingular, $X$ is rational if and only if $X\cong\mathbb{P}^1$ (I, 6.12). We know $p_a(\mathbb{P}^1)=0$ (I, Ex. 7.2), so only the converse needs proof. Suppose $X$ has genus $0$, take two distinct points $P,Q$, and apply Riemann–Roch to $D=P-Q$. By (1.3.3) we have $\deg(K-D)=-2<0$, so $l(K-D)=0$ and hence $l(D)=\deg D+1-g=1$. But $D$ has degree $0$, so (1.2) gives $D\sim0$, that is $P\sim Q$. This implies $X$ is rational (II, 6.10.1).

Example 1.3.6 A curve of genus $1$ is called an elliptic curve. On an elliptic curve the canonical divisor has degree $0$ by (1.3.3); on the other hand $l(K)=p_g=1$, so $K\sim0$ by (1.2).

Example 1.3.7 Let $X$ be an elliptic curve, let $P_0\in X$ be a point, and let $\operatorname{Pic}^{\circ}X$ denote the subgroup of $\operatorname{Pic}X$ corresponding to divisors of degree $0$. Then the map $P\mapsto\mathscr{L}(P-P_0)$ is a bijection between the set of points of $X$ and the group $\operatorname{Pic}^{\circ}X$. So the set of points of $X$ acquires a group structure with $P_0$ as identity, generalizing (II, 6.10.2).

To prove this it is enough to show that for every divisor $D$ of degree $0$ there is a unique point $P\in X$ with $D\sim P-P_0$. Apply Riemann–Roch to $D+P_0$: $$l(D+P_0)-l(K-D-P_0)=1+1-1.$$ Since $\deg K=0$ we have $\deg(K-D-P_0)=-1$, whence $l(K-D-P_0)=0$. So $l(D+P_0)=1$, that is $\dim|D+P_0|=0$, meaning there is exactly one effective divisor linearly equivalent to $D+P_0$. Its degree is $1$, so it must be a single point $P$. This gives the unique $P$ with $P\sim D+P_0$, that is $D\sim P-P_0$.

Remark This argument is the algebraic origin of the group law on an elliptic curve: the group law is not defined by three points being collinear in a picture, but is a direct consequence of the fact — supplied by Riemann–Roch — that every degree-$0$ class contains exactly one $P-P_0$. §4 will show that after embedding $X$ in $\mathbb{P}^2$ by $|3P_0|$, this group law agrees with the geometric description by collinearity.

Remark 1.3.8 For other proofs of Riemann–Roch see Serre [7, Ch. II] and Fulton [1].

8. Summary of this section

A curve means a complete nonsingular integral scheme of dimension $1$ over an algebraically closed field, and is necessarily projective. The genus has three equivalent definitions, all written $g$, and every $g\geq0$ is realized. The dictionary between divisors and invertible sheaves, $\operatorname{Cl}X\cong\operatorname{Pic}X$, is carried over verbatim from II.6; the complete linear system $|D|$ is the projectivization of $H^0(X,\mathscr{L}(D))$, with $\dim|D|=l(D)-1$.

Lemma 1.2 supplies the most frequently used vanishing tool: negative degree means no global sections. Riemann–Roch itself relates $l(D)-l(K-D)$ to $\deg D+1-g$; via Serre duality the proof reduces to an identity about $\chi$, then to a point-by-point induction. Immediate consequences are $\deg K=2g-2$, the nonspeciality of divisors of degree exceeding $2g-2$, the equivalence of rationality with $g=0$, and on an elliptic curve both $K\sim0$ and the group structure on the set of points.

Everything in the following sections is built on these computations: §2 compares the canonical divisors of two curves using $\deg K=2g-2$ to obtain Hurwitz's formula; §3 uses Riemann–Roch to turn the embedding criterion of (II, 7.3) into a dimension condition; §5 uses it to discuss the canonical embedding and Clifford's theorem.

Appendix: external results cited in this section

This section quotes results from the first three chapters extensively. Below, each citation is spelled out, with a note on what it does here. A reader who has not read the relevant chapter can treat these as black boxes.

From Chapter I (varieties)

(I, 3.4)
A projective variety $Y$ over an algebraically closed field satisfies $\Gamma(Y,\mathcal{O}_Y)=k$: the only global regular functions are the constants. This is the algebraic version of the statement that a global holomorphic function on a compact Riemann surface is constant. In the proof of Riemann–Roch it supplies $\dim H^0(X,\mathcal{O}_X)=1$ in the case $D=0$; it is also used in the proof of (II, 7.7) to show that a rational function without zeros or poles is constant.
(I, Ex. 7.2)
Computation of Hilbert polynomials and arithmetic genus. Two facts are used: $p_a(\mathbb{P}^1)=0$, and a nonsingular plane curve of degree $d$ in $\mathbb{P}^2$ satisfies $p_a=\tfrac12(d-1)(d-2)$. The first is used in Example 1.3.5; the second recurs throughout §3 when computing the genus of a plane curve.
(I, Ex. 6.1)
A curve is called rational if it is birationally equivalent to $\mathbb{P}^1$. This is the definition of “rational” used in Example 1.3.5.
(I, 6.12)
A complete nonsingular curve is determined up to isomorphism by its function field: two complete nonsingular curves are birationally equivalent if and only if they are isomorphic. Hence in Example 1.3.5, “rational” can be upgraded directly to “isomorphic to $\mathbb{P}^1$”. The corresponding statement in the Stacks Project is the equivalence between the category of curves and the category of field extensions of transcendence degree $1$ (tag 0BY1).

From Chapter II (schemes)

(II, §6)
The chapter on divisors: Weil divisors, Cartier divisors, the divisor class group $\operatorname{Cl}X$ and $\operatorname{Pic}X$.
(II, 6.7)
A complete nonsingular curve is necessarily projective. This is what makes the tools of II.7 on projective embeddings, and Serre duality (which requires projectivity), directly applicable to the curves of this chapter. The general version in the Stacks Project: a proper scheme of dimension at most $1$ over a field is projective (Varieties, Lemma 33.43.4, in the section with tag 0A22).
(II, 6.10)
On a complete nonsingular curve a principal divisor has degree $0$, so the degree of a divisor depends only on its linear equivalence class. Used in both Lemma 1.2 and Example 1.3.5.
(II, 6.10.1)
If a curve $X$ has two distinct points $P\neq Q$ with $P\sim Q$, then $X\cong\mathbb{P}^1$. This is the last step of Example 1.3.5.
(II, 6.10.2)
The group structure on a plane cubic defined geometrically, by collinearity of three points. Example 1.3.7 re-derives this group structure from Riemann–Roch and extends it to an arbitrary elliptic curve.
(II, 6.16)
For an integral, separated, noetherian, locally factorial scheme (a nonsingular variety satisfies these conditions) there is a group isomorphism $\operatorname{Cl}X\cong\operatorname{Pic}X$ given by $D\mapsto\mathscr{L}(D)$. This is the dictionary between the language of divisors and the language of invertible sheaves.
(II, 6.18)
If $D$ is an effective divisor and $Y$ is the corresponding closed subscheme, then the ideal sheaf of $Y$ is $\mathscr{L}(-D)$; that is, there is an exact sequence $0\to\mathscr{L}(-D)\to\mathcal{O}_X\to\mathcal{O}_Y\to0$. The exact sequence used in the proof of Riemann–Roch, with $D=P$ a single point and $\mathcal{O}_Y=k(P)$, is the special case.
(II, §7)
The chapter on projective morphisms: linear systems, base-point freeness, ample and very ample.
(II, 5.19)
On a projective scheme over a noetherian ring, the space of global sections of a coherent sheaf is a finitely generated module, in particular a finite-dimensional vector space over a field. This guarantees $l(D)<\infty$.
(II, 7.3)
The criterion for a closed immersion: the morphism given by a linear system is a closed immersion if and only if the system separates points and separates tangent vectors. This section does not use it directly, but Proposition 3.1 of §3 is precisely the translation of this criterion into a dimension condition such as $\dim|D-P-Q|=\dim|D|-2$.
(II, 7.7)
Let $D_0$ be a divisor and $\mathscr{L}\cong\mathscr{L}(D_0)$. Then the divisor of zeros of every nonzero section is effective and linearly equivalent to $D_0$; every effective divisor linearly equivalent to $D_0$ arises this way; and two sections have the same divisor of zeros if and only if they differ by a $\lambda\in k^*$. Hence $|D_0|$ is in bijection with $(H^0(X,\mathscr{L}(D_0))-\{0\})/k^*$, carries a projective space structure, and satisfies $\dim|D|=l(D)-1$.
(II, Ex. 7.6)
The Riemann–Roch problem: for a divisor $D$, determine $\dim|nD|$ as a function of $n$, particularly for large $n$. Remark 1.3.2 gives the complete answer in the case of curves.
(II, 8.18.2)
The canonical sheaf and the geometric genus. On a nonsingular variety $\omega_X=\wedge^n\Omega_{X/k}$ with $n=\dim X$, and the geometric genus is $p_g=\dim_k\Gamma(X,\omega_X)$. On a curve $n=1$, so $\omega_X=\Omega_X$.
(II, 7.6.2)
On a nonsingular quadric surface $Q\cong\mathbb{P}^1\times\mathbb{P}^1$, an invertible sheaf of type $(a,b)$ is ample if and only if it is very ample if and only if $a,b>0$. This is not cited in the body of the section; it is listed here to point out that the quadric surface appearing in Remark 1.1.1 is exactly the object studied there.

From Chapter III (cohomology)

(III, 5.2)
For a coherent sheaf on a projective scheme over a noetherian ring, all cohomology groups are finitely generated modules, hence finite-dimensional over a field. This is another source of the finiteness of $l(D)$, and also guarantees that $\chi(\mathscr{F})$ is defined.
(III, Ex. 5.1)
The Euler characteristic $\chi(\mathscr{F})=\sum_i(-1)^i\dim H^i(X,\mathscr{F})$ is additive on short exact sequences of coherent sheaves: if $0\to\mathscr{F}'\to\mathscr{F}\to\mathscr{F}''\to0$ is exact, then $\chi(\mathscr{F})=\chi(\mathscr{F}')+\chi(\mathscr{F}'')$. This is the engine of the inductive step in Riemann–Roch.
(III, Ex. 5.2)
The Hilbert polynomial of a projective scheme $X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^n$ satisfies $P_X(n)=\chi(\mathcal{O}_X(n))$: the Hilbert polynomial is the Euler characteristic after twisting. Remark 1.3.1 uses it to recognize the hyperplane-section case as a special case of Riemann–Roch.
(III, Ex. 5.3)
For a projective scheme of dimension $r$, $p_a(X)=(-1)^r(\chi(\mathcal{O}_X)-1)$. In the curve case $r=1$, substituting $\chi(\mathcal{O}_X)=1-\dim H^1(X,\mathcal{O}_X)$ gives $p_a=\dim H^1(X,\mathcal{O}_X)$, which is the first equality of Proposition 1.1.
(III, Ex. 5.6)
Cohomology of curves of type $(a,b)$ on a nonsingular quadric surface, in particular $p_a=ab-a-b+1$. Taking $(a,b)=(g+1,2)$ gives $p_a=2(g+1)-(g+1)-2+1=g$, which is the source of the claim in Remark 1.1.1 that every genus is realized.
(III, 7.12.1)
Serre duality for curves: if $X$ is a projective curve and $\mathscr{L}$ an invertible sheaf, then $H^0(X,\omega_X\otimes\mathscr{L}^{\vee})$ and $H^1(X,\mathscr{L})$ are dual vector spaces, so $\dim H^1(X,\mathscr{L})=\dim H^0(X,\omega_X\otimes\mathscr{L}^\vee)$. This is the only substantive input to the proof of Riemann–Roch: it reinterprets $l(K-D)$ as $\dim H^1(X,\mathscr{L}(D))$.
(III, 7.12.2)
The consequence of Serre duality that $p_g=\dim H^1(X,\mathcal{O}_X)$, which is the second equality of Proposition 1.1. Taking $\mathscr{L}=\mathcal{O}_X$ makes $H^0(X,\omega_X)$ dual to $H^1(X,\mathcal{O}_X)$, and the dimension of the former is $p_g$.
