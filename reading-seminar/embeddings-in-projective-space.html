Embeddings in projective space — Hartshorne IV.3

source: Hartshorne, Algebraic Geometry, IV.3  ·  prerequisites: IV.1 Riemann–Roch, IV.2 Hurwitz

This section studies embeddings of curves in projective space. There are two main results: every curve embeds in $\mathbb{P}^3$, and every curve maps birationally into $\mathbb{P}^2$ with at worst nodes as singularities. The first is obtained by projecting down one dimension at a time; the second requires controlling the singularities that projection creates, and its proof uses Hurwitz's theorem, with an extra twist in characteristic $p$.

1. The criterion, curve version

First, notation. An invertible sheaf $\mathscr{L}$ on a curve $X$ is very ample if it is isomorphic to the $\mathcal{O}_X(1)$ of some immersion of $X$ into a projective space; it is ample if for every coherent sheaf $\mathscr{F}$ the sheaf $\mathscr{F}\otimes\mathscr{L}^n$ is generated by global sections for $n\gg0$. The two are related by: $\mathscr{L}$ is ample if and only if some $\mathscr{L}^n$ is very ample. A divisor $D$ is called ample or very ample when $\mathscr{L}(D)$ is.

A linear system $\mathfrak{d}$ is a linear subspace of a complete system $|D|$, and a point $P$ is a base point of it if $P\in\operatorname{Supp}D'$ for every $D'\in\mathfrak{d}$; a complete system $|D|$ is base-point free if and only if $\mathscr{L}(D)$ is generated by global sections.

The first result of this section restates the criterion of (II, §7) — when does a linear system give a closed immersion — for curves, replacing “separates points and separates tangent vectors” by a comparison of dimensions.

Proposition 3.1 Let $D$ be a divisor on a curve $X$. Then

(a) the complete linear system $|D|$ is base-point free if and only if for every point $P\in X$, $$\dim|D-P|=\dim|D|-1;$$

(b) $D$ is very ample if and only if for any two points $P,Q\in X$ (including the case $P=Q$), $$\dim|D-P-Q|=\dim|D|-2.$$

Proof

Start from the exact sequence of sheaves $$0\to\mathscr{L}(D-P)\to\mathscr{L}(D)\to k(P)\to 0,$$ and take global sections: $$0\to\Gamma(X,\mathscr{L}(D-P))\to\Gamma(X,\mathscr{L}(D))\to k.$$ So $\dim|D-P|$ equals either $\dim|D|$ or $\dim|D|-1$, and nothing else. On the other hand, sending a divisor $E$ to $E+P$ defines a map $$\varphi:|D-P|\to|D|,$$ which is visibly injective. Hence the two linear systems have equal dimension if and only if $\varphi$ is surjective, and $\varphi$ is surjective if and only if $P$ is a base point of $|D|$. This proves (a).

For (b) we may assume $|D|$ is base-point free. If $D$ is very ample this holds automatically; conversely, if $D$ satisfies the condition in (b) then $\dim|D-P|=\dim|D|-1$ for every $P$, so $|D|$ is base-point free.

Now $|D|$ determines a morphism from $X$ to $\mathbb{P}^n$, and the question is when this morphism is a closed immersion. By the criteria (II, 7.3) and (II, 7.8.2) we must ask whether $|D|$ separates points and separates tangent vectors. The first condition says that for any two distinct points $P,Q$, the point $Q$ is not a base point of $|D-P|$; by (a) this is equivalent to $$\dim|D-P-Q|=\dim|D|-2.$$ The second condition says that for each $P$ there is a $D'\in|D|$ in which $P$ occurs with multiplicity exactly $1$. Indeed $\dim T_P(X)=1$, while $\dim T_P(D')=0$ when $P$ has multiplicity $1$ in $D'$ and $=1$ when the multiplicity is higher. But this says precisely that $P$ is not a base point of $|D-P|$, and one more application of (a) gives $$\dim|D-2P|=\dim|D|-2.$$ The conclusion now follows from (II, 7.3). $\blacksquare$

Remark — how the two conditions divide the labour The half with $P\neq Q$ gives separation of points; the half with $P=Q$ gives separation of tangent vectors. The latter looks unnatural at first, but unwinds cleanly: the condition “$P$ is not a base point of $|D-P|$” says there is a section $s$ which, viewed as a section of $\mathscr{L}(D)$, vanishes at $P$, but which, viewed as a section of $\mathscr{L}(D-P)$, does not vanish at $P$ — that is, $s$ vanishes at $P$ to order exactly $1$. Vanishing to order exactly $1$ is precisely “vanishing to first order but not to second”, which is separation of tangent vectors.

2. Large enough degree is good enough

Proposition 3.1 turned embedding into a comparison of dimensions, and dimensions are given exactly by Riemann–Roch as soon as the divisor is nonspecial. So once the degree is large enough, the criterion holds automatically.

Corollary 3.2 Let $D$ be a divisor on a curve $X$ of genus $g$.

(a) If $\deg D\geq 2g$, then $|D|$ is base-point free.

(b) If $\deg D\geq 2g+1$, then $D$ is very ample.

Proof

In (a) both $D$ and $D-P$ have degree exceeding $2g-2$, so both are nonspecial by (1.3.4), and Riemann–Roch gives $\dim|D-P|=\dim|D|-1$. In (b) both $D$ and $D-P-Q$ are likewise nonspecial, so $\dim|D-P-Q|=\dim|D|-2$. $\blacksquare$

Corollary 3.3 A divisor $D$ on a curve is ample if and only if $\deg D>0$.

Proof

If $D$ is ample, some multiple is very ample, so $nD\sim H$ is the hyperplane section of some projective embedding, and $\deg H>0$, whence $\deg D>0$. Conversely, if $\deg D>0$ then $\deg nD\geq 2g+1$ for $n\gg0$, so $nD$ is very ample by (3.2), and $D$ is ample. $\blacksquare$

Remark — where the work went The proof of Corollary 3.3 is two lines only because the abstract criterion has already been peeled apart layer by layer beforehand: (II, 7.3) gives the criterion for a closed immersion, Proposition 3.1 converts it into a dimension condition, Corollary 3.2 converts that into a degree condition, and by this point nothing is left but “take a power to make the degree large”. That ample equals positive degree is a remarkably clean statement on curves; in higher dimensions it is nothing like this — on a surface one needs the Nakai–Moishezon criterion, controlling $D^2$ and $D\cdot C$ simultaneously.

Example 3.3.1 When $g=0$, a divisor $D$ is ample if and only if it is very ample if and only if $\deg D>0$, since here $X\cong\mathbb{P}^1$.

Example 3.3.2 If $D$ is very ample, with corresponding closed immersion $\varphi:X\to\mathbb{P}^n$, then the degree of the image $\varphi(X)$ as a projective variety equals $\deg D$.

Example 3.3.3 On an elliptic curve, every divisor of degree $3$ is very ample. Such a divisor is nonspecial, and Riemann–Roch gives $\dim|D|=2$, so every elliptic curve embeds in $\mathbb{P}^2$ as a cubic curve. Conversely, every nonsingular plane cubic has genus $1$.

For $g=1$ the conclusion can be sharpened to: $D$ is very ample if and only if $\deg D\geq 3$. For if $\deg D=2$, Riemann–Roch gives $\dim|D|=1$, so $|D|$ defines a morphism from $X$ to $\mathbb{P}^1$, and this cannot be a closed immersion: otherwise $X$ would be isomorphic to a closed subscheme of $\mathbb{P}^1$, which for curves can only be $\mathbb{P}^1$ itself, and the genus does not match.

Example 3.3.4 When $g=2$, every divisor of degree $5$ is very ample and $\dim|D|=3$, so every curve of genus $2$ embeds in $\mathbb{P}^3$ as a curve of degree $5$.

Example 3.3.5 The bound in Corollary 3.2 is not optimal in general. On a plane quartic curve $X$, the hyperplane section $D=X\cdot H$ is very ample with $\deg D=4$, whereas $g=3$ and so $2g+1=7$.

Remark — why the bound is not tight The proof of Corollary 3.2 relies on three divisors being nonspecial simultaneously, and in Example 3.3.5 we have $\deg D=4=2g-2$, which falls just outside the range where the nonspeciality criterion applies. In fact a plane quartic satisfies $\omega_X\cong\mathcal{O}_X(1)$, that is $K\sim D$, so $l(K-D)=1>0$ and $D$ is a special divisor. Special divisors can perfectly well be very ample; the method of Corollary 3.2 simply cannot see it. This curve is in fact the canonical model of a non-hyperelliptic curve of genus $3$, which §5 will treat properly.

3. Projection and dropping the dimension

The next goal is to embed an arbitrary curve in $\mathbb{P}^3$. The tool is projection: take $X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^n$ and a point $O\notin X$, project $X$ from $O$ into $\mathbb{P}^{n-1}$, and ask when this is still a closed immersion.

The construction is as follows. Fix a hyperplane $H\cong\mathbb{P}^{n-1}$ with $O\notin H$, and for each $P\neq O$ set $$\varphi(P)=\overline{OP}\cap H,$$ a point which exists and is unique because a line and a hyperplane in $\mathbb{P}^n$ always meet and $O\notin H$. In coordinates with $O=(0:\cdots:0:1)$ and $H=\{x_n=0\}$ this is just dropping the last coordinate. The hypothesis $O\notin X$ guarantees $\varphi$ is defined everywhere on $X$.

If $P,Q$ are two distinct points of $X$, the line joining them is called a secant; if $P\in X$, the unique line $L\subseteq\mathbb{P}^n$ through $P$ whose tangent space coincides with $T_P(X)$ is called the tangent line to $X$ at $P$.

Proposition 3.4 Let $X$ be a curve in $\mathbb{P}^n$, let $O$ be a point not on $X$, and let $\varphi:X\to\mathbb{P}^{n-1}$ be the morphism determined by projection from $O$. Then $\varphi$ is a closed immersion if and only if

(1) $O$ lies on no secant line of $X$, and

(2) $O$ lies on no tangent line of $X$.

Proof

By (II, 7.8.1), the linear system corresponding to $\varphi$ is the one cut out on $X$ by the hyperplanes of $\mathbb{P}^n$ containing $O$, so $\varphi$ is a closed immersion if and only if this linear system separates points and separates tangent vectors. For two distinct points $P,Q$, separating them requires a hyperplane containing $O$ and $P$ but not $Q$, which is possible exactly when $O$ is not on the line $PQ$. For a point $P$, separating tangent vectors requires a hyperplane containing $O$ and $P$ and meeting $X$ at $P$ with intersection multiplicity $1$, which is possible exactly when $O$ is not on the tangent line at $P$. $\blacksquare$

Remark — the tangent line is a limit of secants The two conditions are two faces of one thing. As $Q$ approaches $P$ along $X$, the limiting position of the secant $\overline{PQ}$ is the tangent line $T_P(X)$. Condition (1) prevents two points from being crushed together by the projection; condition (2) prevents the tangent direction at a single point from being crushed.

Proposition 3.5 If $X$ is a curve in $\mathbb{P}^n$ with $n\geq 4$, then there exists a point $O\notin X$ such that projection from $O$ gives a closed immersion of $X$ into $\mathbb{P}^{n-1}$.

Proof

Write $\operatorname{Sec}X$ for the union of all secant lines, the secant variety of $X$. It is a locally closed subset of $\mathbb{P}^n$ of dimension at most $3$: locally at least, it is the image of the map $$(X\times X-\Delta)\times\mathbb{P}^1\to\mathbb{P}^n$$ sending $(P,Q,t)$ to the point of the secant with parameter $t$.

Write $\operatorname{Tan}X$ for the union of all tangent lines, the tangent variety. It is a closed subset of $\mathbb{P}^n$ of dimension at most $2$, being locally the image of $X\times\mathbb{P}^1$.

Since $n\geq 4$ we get $\operatorname{Sec}X\cup\operatorname{Tan}X\neq\mathbb{P}^n$, so there are plenty of points $O$ lying on no secant and no tangent. By (3.4), projection from such an $O$ gives the desired closed immersion. $\blacksquare$

Remark — why the dimension count does not involve $n$ The bound on the dimension of the secant variety is $1+1+1$: two degrees of freedom to choose the two points, one to move along the secant. For the tangent variety it is $1+1$. These numbers depend only on the dimension of $X$, not on the dimension of the ambient space. So as soon as $n$ exceeds $3$, the bad locus cannot fill up $\mathbb{P}^n$.

Corollary 3.6 Every curve can be embedded in $\mathbb{P}^3$.

Proof

First embed $X$ in some projective space: take a divisor $D$ of degree $d\geq 2g+1$, which is very ample by (3.2), and the complete system $|D|$ gives an embedding of $X$ in $\mathbb{P}^n$ with $n=\dim|D|$. If $n\leq 3$, regard $\mathbb{P}^n$ as a linear subspace of $\mathbb{P}^3$. If $n\geq 4$, apply (3.5) repeatedly, projecting one dimension at a time, until $X$ is embedded in $\mathbb{P}^3$. $\blacksquare$

Remark — $\mathbb{P}^3$ is the limit The argument stops at $n=3$, and this is not a technical shortcoming. The number $\dim\mathbb{P}^3=3$ is exactly the upper bound for the dimension of the secant variety, and for a general curve the secant variety really does fill $\mathbb{P}^3$; so when projecting from $\mathbb{P}^3$ to $\mathbb{P}^2$ one cannot avoid every secant, and the image is necessarily singular. What follows is the study of how to keep those singularities as mild as possible.

4. Projecting to the plane: nodes

When a curve in $\mathbb{P}^3$ is projected to $\mathbb{P}^2$, the secant variety generally fills $\mathbb{P}^3$, so secants cannot be avoided and the image is singular. But the centre of projection $O$ can be chosen so that $\varphi$ is birational onto its image and the image has at worst nodes as singularities.

Recall that a node of a plane curve is a singular point of multiplicity $2$ with two distinct tangent directions. A line in $\mathbb{P}^3$ meeting $X$ in three or more distinct points is called a multisecant. A secant joining $P$ and $Q$ is said to have coplanar tangents if the tangent lines $L_P$ and $L_Q$ lie in a common plane, equivalently if $L_P$ and $L_Q$ meet.

Proposition 3.7 Let $X$ be a curve in $\mathbb{P}^3$, let $O\notin X$, and let $\varphi:X\to\mathbb{P}^2$ be the morphism determined by projection from $O$. Then $\varphi$ is birational onto its image and $\varphi(X)$ has at worst nodes as singularities if and only if

(1) $O$ lies on only finitely many secants of $X$,

(2) $O$ lies on no tangent line of $X$,

(3) $O$ lies on no multisecant of $X$,

(4) $O$ lies on no secant with coplanar tangents.

Proof

Return to the proof of (II, 7.3). Condition (1) says that $\varphi$ is one-to-one almost everywhere, hence birational. When $O$ does lie on some secant, conditions (2), (3), (4) guarantee that this secant meets $X$ in exactly two points $P,Q$, is tangent to $X$ at neither, and that the tangent lines at $P$ and $Q$ map to two distinct lines in $\mathbb{P}^2$. So the image $\varphi(X)$ has a node there. $\blacksquare$

Remark — what each of the four conditions prevents Condition (1) makes the singular points finite in number and gives birationality. Condition (2) prevents a tangent direction from being crushed, which would produce a cusp rather than a node. Condition (3) prevents three points from being crushed to one, which would produce a triple point. Condition (4) prevents the tangent directions of the two branches from coinciding in the image, which again would not be a node. Only all four together confine the singularities to the mildest kind.

To prove that a point $O$ satisfying (1)–(4) exists, one can count dimensions of the bad locus as in (3.5). The difficulty lies in the last two conditions: one needs to know that not every secant is a multisecant, and that not every secant has coplanar tangents. Over the complex numbers this can be seen by differential geometry, but the proof below works in every characteristic, and interestingly uses Hurwitz's theorem.

Proposition 3.8 Let $X$ be a curve in $\mathbb{P}^3$ not contained in any plane. If either

(a) every secant of $X$ is a multisecant, or

(b) for any two points $P,Q\in X$ the tangent lines $L_P,L_Q$ are coplanar,

then there is a point $A\in\mathbb{P}^3$ lying on every tangent line of $X$.

Proof

First we show (a) implies (b). Fix $R\in X$ and consider the morphism $\psi:X-R\to\mathbb{P}^2$ induced by projection from $R$. Since every secant is a multisecant, $\psi$ is many-to-one. If $\psi$ is inseparable, then for every $P\in X$ the tangent line at $P$ passes through $R$, which immediately gives (b) and the conclusion; so we may assume every such $\psi$ is separable. In that case pick a nonsingular point $T$ of $\psi(X)$ over which $\psi$ is unramified. If $P,Q\in\psi^{-1}(T)$, then the tangent lines $L_P,L_Q$ project onto the tangent line $L_T$ of $\psi(X)$ at $T$, so both $L_P$ and $L_Q$ lie in the plane spanned by $R$ and $L_T$, and are therefore coplanar.

This proves: for any $R$, and for almost all $P,Q$ with $P,Q,R$ collinear, the tangent lines $L_P$ and $L_Q$ are coplanar. Hence there is an open subset of $X\times X$ on which pairs of points have coplanar tangents; and “coplanar tangents” is a closed condition, so it holds for all $P,Q\in X$, which is (b).

Now assume (b). Take two points $P,Q\in X$ with distinct tangent lines and set $A=L_P\cap L_Q$. Since $X$ is not contained in any plane, if $\pi$ is the plane spanned by $L_P$ and $L_Q$ then $X\cap\pi$ is a finite set of points. For any $R\in X-X\cap\pi$, the tangent line $L_R$ must meet both $L_P$ and $L_Q$; but $L_R\not\subseteq\pi$, so it must pass through $A$. Thus there is an open subset of $X$ on which points $R$ satisfy $A\in L_R$; since this is a closed condition, it holds for all $R\in X$. $\blacksquare$

Definition A curve $X$ in $\mathbb{P}^n$ is strange if there is a point $A$ lying on all the tangent lines of $X$.

Example 3.8.1 $\mathbb{P}^1$ is strange. The tangent line at any point is $\mathbb{P}^1$ itself, so any $A\in\mathbb{P}^1$ will do.

Example 3.8.2 Over a field of characteristic $2$, a conic in $\mathbb{P}^2$ is strange. Take the conic $y=x^2$; then $dy/dx=2x=0$, so all tangent lines are horizontal and they all pass through the point at infinity on the $x$-axis.

Theorem 3.9 (Samuel) The only strange curves in $\mathbb{P}^n$ are the lines (3.8.1) and the conics in characteristic $2$ (3.8.2).

Proof

Projecting down if necessary (3.5), we may assume $X$ lies in $\mathbb{P}^3$. Choose an $\mathbb{A}^3$ inside $\mathbb{P}^3$ with affine coordinates $x,y,z$ such that

  1. $A$ is the point at infinity on the $x$-axis;
  2. if $A\in X$, its tangent line $L_A$ does not lie in the $xz$-plane;
  3. the $z$-axis does not meet $X$;
  4. $X$ does not meet the line at infinity of the $xz$-plane, except possibly at $A$.

First project from $A$ to the $yz$-plane. Since $A$ lies on every tangent line of $X$, the corresponding morphism from $X$ to $\mathbb{P}^2$ is ramified everywhere. So either the image is a point, in which case $X$ is a line, or the morphism is inseparable (2.2). In the latter case the functions $y$ and $z$ restricted to $X$ lie in $K(X)^p$, where $\operatorname{char}k=p>0$.

Next project from the $z$-axis to the line at infinity $M$ of the $xy$-plane. That is, for each $P\in X$ define $\varphi(P)$ to be the intersection with $M$ of the plane spanned by $P$ and the $z$-axis. This gives a morphism $\varphi:X\to M$ of degree $d=\deg X$. Note that $\varphi$ is ramified exactly at those points where $X$ meets the finite part of the $xz$-plane, and is not ramified at $A$.

Apply Hurwitz's theorem to $\varphi$. For $P\in X\cap xz$-plane, take $u=x-a$ as a local parameter, where $a\in k$, $a\neq 0$; on $M$ take $t=y/x$ as a local parameter at $A$. By (2.2) we must compute $v_P(dt/du)$. Write $x=u+a$, so $t=y(u+a)^{-1}$. Since $y\in K(X)^p$ we have $dy/du=0$, whence $$dt/du=-y(u+a)^{-2}.$$ Now $u+a$ is a unit in the local ring $\mathcal{O}_P$, so $$v_P(dt/du)=v_P(y).$$ Let $P_1,\dots,P_r$ be all the finite intersections of $X$ with the $xz$-plane. Then Hurwitz's theorem gives $$2g-2=-2d+\sum_{i=1}^{r}v_{P_i}(y).$$

There are two cases.

Case 1: $A\notin X$. Then the $xz$-plane meets $X$ only at the $P_i$. That plane is defined by $y=0$, so the degree of $X$ can be computed by its intersection with it: $$d=\sum_{i=1}^{r}v_{P_i}(y).$$ Substituting gives $2g-2=-d$, which is possible only for $g=0$ and $d=2$. So $X$ is isomorphic as an abstract curve to $\mathbb{P}^1$ (1.3.5), its embedding is given by a divisor $D$ of degree $2$, and Riemann–Roch gives $\dim|D|=2$, so $X$ is a conic in some plane $\mathbb{P}^2$. For a conic to be strange we must have $\operatorname{char}k=2$.

Case 2: $A\in X$. By condition (2), the $xz$-plane crosses $X$ transversally at $A$, so likewise $$d=\sum_{i=1}^{r}v_{P_i}(y)+1,$$ and substituting gives $2g-2=-d-1$, which forces $g=0$ and $d=1$, that is, $X$ is a line. $\blacksquare$

Remark — the hinge of the proof The key step is translating a geometric condition into an algebraic one: “$A$ lies on every tangent line” becomes “ramified everywhere”, then via (2.2) — a separable morphism has only finitely many ramification points — becomes “$y,z\in K(X)^p$”. After that, $dy=0$ leaves only one term in the computation of $dt/du$, the order of ramification is computed as the order of vanishing of $y$, and that in turn is counted by an intersection number. Finally the signs in Hurwitz's formula trap $d$ in a very small range. This is the same kind of argument as the proof that $\mathbb{P}^1$ is simply connected (2.5.3), and as the count of six branch points in (2.2)(a). In characteristic $0$ inseparability does not exist, the argument stops at the first step, and the only strange curves are the lines.

Theorem 3.10 Let $X$ be a curve in $\mathbb{P}^3$. Then there is a point $O\notin X$ such that the morphism $\varphi$ determined by projection from $O$ is a birational morphism from $X$ onto its image in $\mathbb{P}^2$, and that image has at worst nodes as singularities.

Proof

If $X$ already lies in a plane, take any $O$ off that plane. So assume $X$ is contained in no plane. Then $X$ is neither a line nor a conic, so by (3.9) it is not strange, and hence by (3.8) it has a secant which is not a multisecant, and also a secant whose tangents are not coplanar. Nearby secants have the same property, so there is an open subset of $X\times X$ whose pairs $\langle P,Q\rangle$ determine secants that are neither multisecants nor have coplanar tangents. Therefore the pairs whose secant is a multisecant or has coplanar tangents form a proper subset of dimension at most $1$, and the corresponding secants sweep out a subset of $\mathbb{P}^3$ of dimension at most $2$. Together with the tangent variety, of dimension at most $2$ (3.5), this shows there is an open subset of $\mathbb{P}^3$ whose points satisfy (2), (3), (4) of (3.7).

It remains to show that $O$ can be chosen to lie on only finitely many secants. Consider the locally defined map $$(X\times X-\Delta)\times\mathbb{P}^1\to\mathbb{P}^3$$ sending $\langle P,Q,t\rangle$ to the point of the secant with parameter $t$. If the image has dimension less than $3$, take $O$ off every secant. If the image has dimension exactly $3$, this is a morphism between varieties of the same dimension, so by (II, Ex. 3.7) there is an open subset of $\mathbb{P}^3$ over which the fibres are finite, and the points there lie on only finitely many secants. $\blacksquare$

Corollary 3.11 Every curve is birationally equivalent to a plane curve with at worst nodes as singularities.

Proof

Combine (3.6) and (3.10). $\blacksquare$

Remark — why “birationally” cannot be dropped The genus of a smooth plane curve is locked to its degree by $\tfrac12(d-1)(d-2)$, which takes only the values $0,1,3,6,10,\dots$; so curves of genus $2$, $4$, $5$ cannot be isomorphic to any smooth plane curve. Once nodes are allowed the genus formula becomes $\tfrac12(d-1)(d-2)-r$, and subtracting the number $r$ of nodes makes every genus attainable — at the price of asking only for birational equivalence rather than isomorphism.

Remark 3.11.1 By (3.11), one route to studying all curves is to study the family of plane curves of degree $d$ with $r$ nodes. The plane curves of degree $d$ form a linear system of dimension $\tfrac12 d(d+3)$, parametrized by the projective space of that dimension. Inside it, the irreducible curves with $r$ nodes form a locally closed subset $V_{d,r}$. If $X$ is such a curve, the genus of its normalization $\tilde X$ is given by (Ex. 1.8) as $$g=\tfrac12(d-1)(d-2)-r,$$ so $V_{d,r}$ can be nonempty only if $$0\leq r\leq\tfrac12(d-1)(d-2).$$ Both endpoints occur. By Bertini's theorem there are irreducible nonsingular curves of degree $d$ in $\mathbb{P}^2$ for every $d$, giving $r=0$; on the other hand, for any $d$ one can embed $\mathbb{P}^1$ in $\mathbb{P}^d$ as a curve of degree $d$ (Ex. 3.4) and then project to $\mathbb{P}^2$ using (3.5) and (3.10), obtaining a curve of degree $d$ with only nodes and $g(\tilde X)=0$, which gives $r=\tfrac12(d-1)(d-2)$.

The general structure of $V_{d,r}$ is considerably harder. Severi asserted that for every $d,r$ satisfying the inequality above the algebraic set $V_{d,r}$ is nonempty and irreducible of dimension $\tfrac12 d(d+3)-r$, but a complete proof was not given until Joe Harris.

5. Summary of this section

Proposition 3.1 translates the closed-immersion criterion of (II, 7.3) into dimension conditions: base-point freeness is $\dim|D-P|=\dim|D|-1$, and very ampleness is $\dim|D-P-Q|=\dim|D|-2$, where $P\neq Q$ corresponds to separating points and $P=Q$ to separating tangent vectors. Corollary 3.2 then uses nonspeciality to reduce these to the degree conditions $\deg D\geq 2g$ and $\deg D\geq 2g+1$, and Corollary 3.3 concludes that on a curve, ample is equivalent to positive degree.

The embedding into $\mathbb{P}^3$ comes from projecting one dimension at a time: Proposition 3.4 says projection from $O$ is a closed immersion if and only if $O$ lies on no secant and no tangent, while the secant variety has dimension at most $3$ and the tangent variety at most $2$; so for $n\geq 4$ the bad locus cannot fill $\mathbb{P}^n$, and repeated projection gives Corollary 3.6. $\mathbb{P}^3$ is the limit of this method, because the upper bound for the dimension of the secant variety is exactly $3$.

Projecting further to $\mathbb{P}^2$, one can only ask for birationality with at worst nodes in the image, characterized by the four conditions of Proposition 3.7. Proving that such a centre exists requires ruling out “every secant is a multisecant” and “any two tangents are coplanar”; Proposition 3.8 shows both of these force the curve to be strange, and Theorem 3.9 shows the only strange curves are lines and conics in characteristic $2$. Theorem 3.10 and Corollary 3.11 follow.

Later, §5 returns to the canonical linear system, proves that the canonical map of a non-hyperelliptic curve is an embedding, and handles special divisors with Clifford's theorem; §6 uses the results of this section to classify curves of low degree in $\mathbb{P}^3$.
