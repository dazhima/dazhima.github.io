Hurwitz's theorem — Hartshorne IV.2

source: Hartshorne, Algebraic Geometry, IV.2  ·  prerequisites: IV.1 Riemann–Roch

This section studies finite morphisms $f:X\to Y$ between curves and examines the relation between the canonical divisors of the two curves. The resulting formula ties the genus of $X$, the genus of $Y$ and the ramification together, and is called Hurwitz's theorem. It is one of the most frequently used computational tools of this chapter: §3 will use it to identify strange curves, and §4 to analyse double covers of elliptic curves.

1. The ramification index

The degree of a finite morphism $f:X\to Y$ of curves is defined as the degree $[K(X):K(Y)]$ of the function field extension (II, §6).

For a point $P\in X$, set $Q=f(P)$ and take $t\in\mathcal{O}_Q$ to be a local parameter at $Q$. Regarding $t$ as an element of $\mathcal{O}_P$ via the natural map $f^{\#}:\mathcal{O}_Q\to\mathcal{O}_P$, define the ramification index at $P$ to be $$e_P=v_P(t),$$ where $v_P$ is the valuation of the valuation ring $\mathcal{O}_P$. If $e_P>1$ we say $f$ is ramified at $P$ and call $Q$ a branch point of $f$; if $e_P=1$ we say $f$ is unramified at $P$. Since the base field $k$ is algebraically closed, every point has $k(P)=k(Q)$, so this definition agrees with the earlier definition of unramified (III, Ex. 10.3). In particular, if $f$ is unramified everywhere then it is étale, since it is flat in any case (III, 9.7).

Remark — what the ramification index counts $e_P$ measures how many times $f$ “winds around” near $P$. Taking a local parameter $t$ at $Q$ and a local parameter $u$ at $P$, the morphism pulls $t$ back locally to $u^{e_P}$ times a unit, so it looks like $z\mapsto z^{e}$. The counterpart in complex analysis is the multiplicity of a meromorphic map at a point, with $e_P=1$ meaning locally injective. The number of points in a fibre is related to the ramification indices by $\sum_{P\mapsto Q}e_P=\deg f$: counted with multiplicity, every fibre has exactly $\deg f$ points, a fact already used repeatedly.

If $\operatorname{char}k=0$, or if $\operatorname{char}k=p$ and $p\nmid e_P$, the ramification at that point is called tame; if $p\mid e_P$ it is called wild. The distinction is essential in characteristic $p$, and we will see that wild ramification turns the formula into an inequality.

We have already defined the homomorphism of divisor groups $f^*:\operatorname{Div}Y\to\operatorname{Div}X$, $$f^*(Q)=\sum_{P\mapsto Q}e_P\cdot P,$$ extended by linearity (II, §6). If $D$ is a divisor on $Y$ then $f^*(\mathscr{L}(D))\cong\mathscr{L}(f^*D)$ (II, Ex. 6.8), so $f^*$ on divisors is compatible with $f^*$ on invertible sheaves, $\operatorname{Pic}Y\to\operatorname{Pic}X$.

2. The exact sequence of differentials

A morphism $f:X\to Y$ is separable if $K(X)$ is a separable field extension of $K(Y)$.

Proposition 2.1 Let $f:X\to Y$ be a finite separable morphism of curves. Then there is an exact sequence on $X$, $$0\to f^*\Omega_Y\to\Omega_X\to\Omega_{X/Y}\to0.$$

Proof

The sequence is already available from (II, 8.11), except that the left-hand end need not be zero, so it suffices to prove that $f^*\Omega_Y\to\Omega_X$ is injective. Both are invertible sheaves on $X$, so it is enough to show the map is nonzero at the generic point. But $K(X)$ is separable over $K(Y)$, so $\Omega_{X/Y}$ vanishes at the generic point of $X$ (II, 8.6A), whence $f^*\Omega_Y\to\Omega_X$ is surjective there, and in particular nonzero. $\blacksquare$

Remark — why checking the generic point is enough For a map between invertible sheaves, if it is nonzero somewhere then by integrality it is nonzero at the generic point; and invertible sheaves are torsion free, so a map that is nonzero at the generic point is injective everywhere. This is the standard device on a curve for reducing a global question to a question at the generic point, and it depends on the assumption that $X$ is integral.

$\Omega_Y$ and $\Omega_X$ correspond to the canonical divisors of $Y$ and $X$, so the sheaf of relative differentials $\Omega_{X/Y}$ measures exactly the difference between them. Let us study this sheaf. For a point $P\in X$ with $Q=f(P)$, take $t$ a local parameter at $Q$ and $u$ a local parameter at $P$. Then $dt$ generates the free $\mathcal{O}_Q$-module $\Omega_{Y,Q}$, and $du$ generates the free $\mathcal{O}_P$-module $\Omega_{X,P}$ (II, 8.7) and (II, 8.8). Hence there is a unique $g\in\mathcal{O}_P$ with $$f^*dt=g\cdot du,$$ and this element is written $dt/du$.

Proposition 2.2 Let $f:X\to Y$ be a finite separable morphism of curves. Then

(a) $\Omega_{X/Y}$ is a torsion sheaf on $X$ whose support is the set of ramification points of $f$; in particular $f$ is ramified at only finitely many points;

(b) for each $P\in X$ the stalk $(\Omega_{X/Y})_P$ is a principal $\mathcal{O}_P$-module of finite length, equal to $v_P(dt/du)$;

(c) if $f$ is tamely ramified at $P$ then $\operatorname{length}(\Omega_{X/Y})_P=e_P-1$; if the ramification is wild then the length is greater than $e_P-1$.

Proof

(a) By (2.1) both $f^*\Omega_Y$ and $\Omega_X$ are invertible sheaves on $X$, so the quotient is a torsion sheaf. And $(\Omega_{X/Y})_P=0$ if and only if $f^*dt$ generates $\Omega_{X,P}$, which happens if and only if $t$ is a local parameter of $\mathcal{O}_P$, that is, if and only if $f$ is unramified at $P$.

(b) The exact sequence of (2.1) gives $(\Omega_{X/Y})_P\cong\Omega_{X,P}/f^*\Omega_{Y,Q}$, which as an $\mathcal{O}_P$-module is isomorphic to $\mathcal{O}_P/(dt/du)$.

(c) Let $e=e_P$ be the ramification index, so that $t=au^e$ with $a\in\mathcal{O}_P$ a unit. Differentiating, $$dt=aeu^{e-1}du+u^e\,da.$$ If the ramification is tame then $e$ is nonzero in $k$, so the first term has valuation $e-1$ while the second has valuation at least $e$, whence $v_P(dt/du)=e-1$. Otherwise $e$ is zero in $k$, the first term vanishes, and $v_P(dt/du)\geq e$. $\blacksquare$

Remark — where wild ramification comes from When the characteristic $p$ divides $e$, the derivative $eu^{e-1}$ of $u^e$ becomes zero: the differential cannot “see” that part of the ramification, and the length only goes up. This is why wild ramification degrades Hurwitz's formula into an inequality. In characteristic $0$ the situation does not arise and all ramification is tame.

3. The ramification divisor and the comparison of canonical divisors

Definition Let $f:X\to Y$ be a finite separable morphism of curves. The ramification divisor of $f$ is $$R=\sum_{P\in X}\operatorname{length}(\Omega_{X/Y})_P\cdot P.$$ By (2.2)(a) this is a finite sum, so it really is a divisor; by (2.2)(c), in the tame case its coefficients are $e_P-1$.

Proposition 2.3 Let $f:X\to Y$ be a finite separable morphism of curves, and let $K_X$ and $K_Y$ be canonical divisors of $X$ and $Y$. Then $$K_X\sim f^*K_Y+R.$$

Proof

Regard $R$ as a closed subscheme of $X$; by (2.2) its structure sheaf $\mathcal{O}_R$ is isomorphic to $\Omega_{X/Y}$. Tensoring the exact sequence of (2.1) by $\Omega_X^{-1}$ gives $$0\to f^*\Omega_Y\otimes\Omega_X^{-1}\to\mathcal{O}_X\to\mathcal{O}_R\to0.$$ The ideal sheaf of $R$ is isomorphic to $\mathscr{L}(-R)$ (II, 6.18), so $$f^*\Omega_Y\otimes\Omega_X^{-1}\cong\mathscr{L}(-R).$$ Passing to the corresponding divisors gives the result. Alternatively, one can prove the proposition by applying the $\det$ operation of (II, Ex. 6.11) to the exact sequence of (2.1). $\blacksquare$

Remark — what the formula says Read it as $K_X-f^*K_Y=R$: the difference between the canonical divisors of the two curves is contributed entirely by ramification. If $f$ is unramified everywhere then $K_X=f^*K_Y$ and pulling back differential forms creates no extra zeros; at each ramification point the pulled-back differential acquires extra zeros, of exactly the order given by the coefficient of $R$. The computation $dt=aeu^{e-1}du$ in (2.2)(c) displays this directly: after pullback there is an extra factor $u^{e-1}$.

4. Hurwitz's theorem

Corollary 2.4 (Hurwitz) Let $f:X\to Y$ be a finite separable morphism of curves and $n=\deg f$. Then $$2g(X)-2=n\cdot\big(2g(Y)-2\big)+\deg R.$$ Moreover, if $f$ has only tame ramification, then $$\deg R=\sum_{P\in X}(e_P-1).$$

Proof

Take degrees in (2.3). The degree of a canonical divisor is $2g-2$ (1.3.3); $f^*$ multiplies degrees by $n$ (II, 6.9); and in the tame case the degree of $R$ is $\sum(e_P-1)$ by (2.2). $\blacksquare$

Remark — the three inputs The formula splices together three things: $\deg K=2g-2$ comes from Riemann–Roch in §1; $\deg f^*D=n\deg D$ comes from the fact that a fibre has $n$ points counted with multiplicity; $\deg R=\sum(e_P-1)$ comes from the local computation with differentials. So Hurwitz is the composite of §1 with the local analysis of this section. In complex geometry it is the Riemann–Hurwitz formula, and is the same statement as the behaviour of the Euler characteristic of a compact surface under a covering, $\chi(X)=n\chi(Y)-\sum(e_P-1)$, bearing in mind that $\chi=2-2g$.

5. Frobenius and the purely inseparable case

Everything above assumed separability. To complete the picture of finite morphisms we must handle the purely inseparable case, and for that we first define the Frobenius morphism.

Definition Let $X$ be a scheme all of whose local rings have characteristic $p$ (that is, contain $\mathbb{Z}/p$). The Frobenius morphism $F:X\to X$ is defined as follows: on the topological space $F$ is the identity map, and $F^{\#}:\mathcal{O}_X\to\mathcal{O}_X$ is the $p$-th power map. Since the local rings have characteristic $p$, $F^{\#}$ induces a local homomorphism on each local ring, so $F$ is indeed a morphism.

Remark 2.4.1 If $\pi:X\to\operatorname{Spec}k$ is a scheme over a field $k$ of characteristic $p$, then $F:X\to X$ is not $k$-linear; rather it commutes with the Frobenius of $\operatorname{Spec}k$, that is, with the $p$-th power map of $k$. To obtain a $k$-linear version, define a new scheme $X_p$ to be the same $X$ but with structure morphism $F\circ\pi$, so that $k$ acts on $\mathcal{O}_{X_p}$ through $p$-th powers. Then $F$ becomes a $k$-linear morphism $F':X_p\to X$, called the $k$-linear Frobenius morphism.

Example 2.4.2 $X_p$ need not be isomorphic to $X$ as a $k$-scheme, but for $X=\operatorname{Spec}k[t]$ with $k$ perfect they are isomorphic, because the $p$-th power map $k\to k$ is a bijection. Under this identification $F':X\to X$ corresponds to the ring homomorphism $k[t]\to k[t]$, $t\mapsto t^p$.

Example 2.4.3 If $X$ is a curve over an algebraically closed field $k$ of characteristic $p$, then $F':X_p\to X$ is a finite morphism of degree $p$, corresponding to the field extension $K\hookrightarrow K^{1/p}$, where $K$ is the function field of $X$.

Proposition 2.5 Let $f:X\to Y$ be a finite morphism of curves with $K(X)$ a purely inseparable extension of $K(Y)$. Then $X$ and $Y$ are isomorphic as abstract schemes, and $f$ is a composite of $k$-linear Frobenius morphisms. In particular $g(X)=g(Y)$.

Proof

Let $\deg f=p^r$; then $K(X)^{p^r}\subseteq K(Y)$, that is $K(X)\subseteq K(Y)^{1/p^r}$. On the other hand consider the composite of $k$-linear Frobenius morphisms $$Y_{p^r}\to Y_{p^{r-1}}\to\cdots\to Y_p\to Y,$$ a morphism $f':Y_{p^r}\to Y$ of degree $p^r$. Since $K(X)\subseteq K(Y)^{1/p^r}$ and both have the same degree over $K(Y)$, we get $K(X)=K(Y)^{1/p^r}$. A curve is determined by its function field (I, 6.12), so $X\cong Y_{p^r}$ and $f=f'$. Hence $X$ and $Y$ are isomorphic as abstract schemes, and the genus does not depend on the $k$-structure, so it is the same. $\blacksquare$

Example 2.5.1 If $X=Y_p$ and $f$ is the $k$-linear Frobenius, then $f$ is ramified everywhere with all ramification indices equal to $p$. Indeed $f$ is the identity on points and the $p$-th power map on the structure sheaf, so a local parameter $t$ pulls back to $t^p$. And $d(t^p)=0$, so $f^*\Omega_Y\to\Omega_X$ is the zero map and $\Omega_{X/Y}\cong\Omega_X$. This does not contradict the separability hypothesis of (2.1); on the contrary, it shows exactly how that exact sequence fails in the purely inseparable case.

Example 2.5.2 If $f:X\to Y$ is separable, then the degree of the ramification divisor $R$ is always even, as one sees directly from the formula (2.4), since every other term there is even.

Example 2.5.3 An étale covering of a scheme $Y$ is a scheme $X$ together with a finite étale morphism $f:X\to Y$; the covering is trivial if $X$ is isomorphic to a disjoint union of finitely many copies of $Y$, and $Y$ is simply connected if it has no nontrivial étale covering.

We now show $\mathbb{P}^1$ is simply connected. Let $f:X\to\mathbb{P}^1$ be an étale covering; we may assume $X$ connected. Since $f$ is étale, $X$ is smooth over $k$ (III, 10.1); since $f$ is finite, $X$ is proper over $k$; so $X$ is a curve (connected plus regular implies irreducible). Étale implies separable, so Hurwitz's theorem applies. Being unramified gives $R=0$, hence $$2g(X)-2=n\cdot(-2).$$ Since $g(X)\geq0$, the only possibility is $g(X)=0$ and $n=1$, so $X=\mathbb{P}^1$.

Remark — the shape of this argument The left-hand side satisfies $2g(X)-2\geq-2$ and the right-hand side satisfies $-2n\leq-2$; squeezing the two forces $n=1$. Most conclusions of the form “every covering is trivial” are read off from the sign constraints in Hurwitz's formula this way: when $g(Y)=0$ the right-hand side plunges as $n$ grows, while the left-hand side is bounded below, so $n$ cannot be large.

Example 2.5.4 If $f:X\to Y$ is any finite morphism of curves, then $g(X)\geq g(Y)$. Decompose the field extension $K(Y)\subseteq K(X)$ into a separable extension followed by a purely inseparable one; by (2.5) the purely inseparable part does not change the genus, so we may assume $f$ separable. If $g(Y)=0$ there is nothing to prove, so assume $g(Y)\geq1$ and rewrite (2.4) as $$g(X)=g(Y)+(n-1)\big(g(Y)-1\big)+\tfrac12\deg R.$$ The claim follows from $n-1\geq0$, $g(Y)-1\geq0$ and $\deg R\geq0$. Incidentally, in the separable case equality holds only when $n=1$, or when $g(Y)=1$ and $f$ is unramified.

Example 2.5.5 (Lüroth's theorem) If $L$ is a subfield of the purely transcendental extension $k(t)$ containing $k$, then $L$ is also purely transcendental. We may assume $L\neq k$, so $L$ has transcendence degree $1$ over $k$ and is therefore the function field of some curve $Y$, and the inclusion $L\subseteq k(t)$ corresponds to a finite morphism $f:\mathbb{P}^1\to Y$. By (2.5.4) we get $g(Y)=0$, and by (1.3.5) $Y\cong\mathbb{P}^1$, so $L\cong k(u)$.

Remark This proof only works over an algebraically closed $k$, but the theorem holds over any field. The analogous statement in dimension $2$ over an algebraically closed field is also true (V, 6.2.1); in dimension $3$ it fails, since there are unirational threefolds that are not rational — see the work of Clemens–Griffiths and of Iskovskih–Manin.

6. Summary of this section

The ramification index $e_P=v_P(t)$ measures the local winding number, and fibres satisfy $\sum e_P=\deg f$. In the separable case there is an exact sequence $0\to f^*\Omega_Y\to\Omega_X\to\Omega_{X/Y}\to0$, whose quotient is a torsion sheaf supported on the ramification points, of local length $e_P-1$ in the tame case and larger in the wild case. This yields the comparison of canonical divisors $K_X\sim f^*K_Y+R$, and taking degrees gives Hurwitz's formula $$2g(X)-2=n\big(2g(Y)-2\big)+\deg R.$$

The purely inseparable case is completely described by Frobenius, and there the genus is unchanged. The typical use of Hurwitz's formula is to read a conclusion off its sign constraints: the simple connectedness of $\mathbb{P}^1$, the fact that a finite morphism cannot lower the genus, and Lüroth's theorem are all obtained this way.

Later, §3 uses it to prove that the only strange curves are lines and conics in characteristic $2$ (3.9), and §4 uses it to show that a double cover of $\mathbb{P}^1$ by an elliptic curve has exactly four branch points, which is what makes the $\lambda$ and $j$ invariants possible.
